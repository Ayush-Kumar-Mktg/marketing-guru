# Positioning and brand playbook

Deep reference for the positioning-and-brand skill.

---

## 1. Positioning in one page

Positioning is the place you hold in a buyer's head relative to the alternatives. If you don't choose it, the market (and now AI assistants) will choose it for you, usually as "one of several similar tools".

### Lead with a familiar category, then differentiate
A stranger's brain needs a folder to file you in. "We're creating a new category" gives them nothing. Start with what they know, then narrow:
- "Email marketing for online stores" (I know what this is) "built only for after-purchase emails" (I know whether I need it).
- Anchoring comparisons work: "like [known tool], but for [specific audience or job]".

### Be specific enough to be obvious
Being the clear answer to a narrow question beats being a plausible answer to a broad one. "The CRM for 10 to 50 person B2B software teams" is easier to choose, easier to remember and easier for AI assistants to recommend than "a CRM for businesses of all sizes."

### Have a point of view
Features get copied. Beliefs are harder to copy. A real point of view is a stated position on how things should work that not everyone agrees with.
- Platitude: "We believe in customer success."
- Point of view: "Spray-and-pray outbound is finished. Only signal-led, account-specific outreach works now."

The test: a real point of view implies things you should stop doing, not only things you should do. If everyone nods, it's consensus. If some people push back, it's working.

### Name the problem
Category creators often start by giving a fuzzy problem a name buyers recognise, then building content, community and product around that name. It's slow and needs patience, but whoever defines the problem sets the rules of competition.

The steps:
1. Find a problem existing categories handle badly.
2. Name it in words your buyers would use.
3. Build content and conversation around the name.
4. Position the product as the thing built for exactly that problem.
5. Own the definition before others arrive.

### The swap test and positioning sentence
See the copywriting skill. In short: if a competitor's logo fits your homepage, you have no positioning. Draft from: For [person] who [problem], [product] is the [category] that [outcome], because [proof only we have].

## 2. How strangers read you

You are the architect who knows every room. Your buyer is a driver passing by at speed. The better you know your product, the worse you are at seeing it fresh.

On first contact, people make three fast judgements:

### Filter: "What can I ignore, and what is this?"
- Keep structure boringly familiar: logo left, nav right, normal buttons, normal footer. Familiar things get filed as background so the message can stand out.
- Let only two or three things break the pattern: a headline that names a specific painful moment, one visual that shows the real problem or product, and proof with a specific outcome.
- Then close two loops quickly: what category this is and why it's relevant to them.
- Ultra-minimal, unconventional pages often create more friction, because nothing can be filed quickly.
- Three-second test: what category is this? Is it relevant to a problem I have? What would I learn by reading on?

### Commit: "Is this worth my effort right now?"
People take the easiest path, not the best one.
- Fewer decisions (default the common option).
- Split big asks into small wins ("add your company name" beats "complete your profile").
- Reveal features gradually.
- Nudge honestly: real usage numbers, a curiosity gap, real scarcity used sparingly.

### Return: "Will I come back?"
Every touch deposits or withdraws trust.
- Clear feedback ("Your workspace is ready. Start here:" beats "Success!").
- Reassurance ("Most teams start here", "You can change this anytime").
- Their success before your growth goal (one useful action before asking for invites).
- Small delights (a short personal welcome, a "what's confusing?" check-in on day three).
- People remember peaks and endings. Design the ending of every flow.

Watch people who have never seen your product use it. It's uncomfortable. That discomfort is the gap between your map and their territory.

## 3. Brand identity foundations

Brand is more than a logo and less mystical than case studies of famous companies make it sound. Four parts, all pulling in the same direction:

### Mission
One bold line, often starting with "To...", describing how the world or your industry looks if you fully succeed. It guides decisions on product, audience, hiring and partners. It doesn't need to mention your product. Prompt: "If we completely succeed, what is different?"

### Story
A short narrative about why you exist. Every story needs tension: a problem, an enemy, a status quo worth fighting.
- **Origin-led:** works when the founders' path is unusual or moving.
- **Customer-led:** works when the origin is ordinary. Put the customer's need at the centre.

Either way, tie the company's success to the customer's. Keep it under about 250 words. Prompts: what real need inspired this? What value does it give people? Why choose us over the obvious alternative?

### Voice
How the company would sound as a person, everywhere.
- One adjective ("casual") is not a voice. Write three do's and three don'ts with examples.
- Avoid "authentic" as a voice trait. It gives no direction and licenses everyone's personal tone.
- A brand archetype (the rebel, the sage, the caregiver, the jester...) helps define your role in the customer's life.
- A "brand muse" (a well-known character whose attitude matches yours) gives the team a quick test: would our muse say this?

### Design basics
- A recognisable logo
- 2 to 4 brand colours
- 2 to 3 typefaces that work together
- Icon style
- Guidance and examples for imagery, photography and texture
- A website that brings it together

Early on, do mission, story and voice yourself and keep design spend modest. A "minimum viable brand" is fine until you've proven the business. Then invest in craft.

## 4. Own your truth

Honesty, especially with wit, gets past scepticism that polished claims can't.
- **Lean into an unflattering truth with humour.** A budget brand that winks at its own imperfections, or a toy store that jokes about stepping on bricks, earns affection a defensive brand never will.
- **Join the joke.** When the internet makes a good-natured meme out of your brand, play along rather than issuing a statement. You can't fight a wildfire with a press release.
- **Radical transparency resets trust.** When the product truly was bad, admitting it publicly, showing the fix and inviting people to judge again can do more than any campaign.
- **Specificity filters.** A cheap, chaotic hostel that markets itself as exactly that attracts the people who want that and repels those who'd leave bad reviews. Nobody craves the buffet. Pick the dish you do best.

## 5. Rebrands: when and how

### Good reasons
- Your positioning has outgrown the brand.
- You're being mistaken for someone else.
- You're targeting a new segment (without abandoning the one that pays you).
- The early brand was a placeholder and you can now afford craft.
- A big new stage: new market, flagship product.

### Lessons from rebrands that went badly
- **Weird needs a reason.** Memorable is useless if people don't remember what you sell.
- **Don't delete your recognisable assets.** Shoppers find products by colour, shape and symbol. Replacing a famous visual cue with a generic modern look can make you invisible on the shelf and look like the cheap option.
- **Keep your strongest equity.** If one name or asset holds more trust than everything else combined, build on it rather than diluting it.
- **Rebrand for the customer you have, not the one you wish you had.** Removing what loyal customers came for (for example, their beloved deals) in pursuit of a different audience can lose both.
- **Bold change still needs focus.** Changing everything at once with vague messaging creates whiplash. Bridge heritage and the new direction.
- **Clarity first, creativity second.**

## 6. Standing out when everyone copies the same playbook

When every company runs the same content, outreach, webinars and ads, individually sensible copying makes everything look the same. Three layers:

1. **Clear messaging and pricing.** Can a buyer answer in 15 seconds: what do you do, who is it for, what does it cost or what's the next step? Without this, creativity is wasted.
2. **A strong point of view** (section 1).
3. **Creative execution:**
   - **Content:** would this piece exist if we didn't make it? If the answer is yes, why make it?
   - **Ads:** break the feed's visual habits (an unexpected style, long copy where everyone is short, a static where everyone uses video).
   - **Outreach:** do something that can't be automated: a personal video, a relevant physical book with a handwritten note, a reference only someone who did real research would know.
   - **Events:** an unusual format (debate, live experiment, workshop), hook ("bring your hardest unsolved problem") or guest (a practitioner who built something real).

**The anti-automation principle:** anything that can be done at scale will soon be done by everyone and stop working. Find the moments where human effort, creativity or physical presence gives you an unfair advantage. Automate the rest.

## 7. AI and differentiation

- AI models predict the most likely output. They're consensus machines. Ask for "copy for our software" and you get the most average copy in the category. When a whole category uses the same tools with similar prompts, its marketing converges.
- Good uses: variations for testing, adapting a human-written core message, research and synthesis, first drafts a human edits, technical tasks (metadata, alt text, schema).
- Bad uses: inventing positioning, producing a recognisable voice, deciding what you should own, deciding what not to say.
- **Brief first.** Before any AI output, write one paragraph: what is the different thing we're saying, and why does it matter to this audience? Give it to the AI as a constraint and judge output against it, not against "sounds fine".
- **Design floor, design edge.** AI raised the floor: competent design is available to everyone. Untouched template output now signals "we didn't care". The edge moves to visible care: custom illustration, recognisable type, considered detail, anything that clearly wasn't a default. Use AI for production (resizing, variations, formats). Keep humans on identity-level decisions. Invest in at least one brand element a template can't easily copy.

## 8. Brand as a business asset

- Brand returns are real but delayed and hard to attribute, so they get underfunded. Teams that optimise only for this quarter's leads often pay more per customer later as brand preference fades.
- **Founder-led marketing:** in crowded categories, a founder who has shared a genuine point of view in public for a year or more has built more trust than most campaigns. The product becomes the physical form of that point of view.
- **Community as distribution:** find an underserved group (a job function, a type of practitioner), create a gathering point or resource for them, and let trust and reach grow there. Members become customers and advocates.
- **The long game:** a small, specific audience often beats a large, general one. Research that helps the whole industry (competitors included) builds real authority.

## 9. Positioning and brand checklist

- [ ] Category stated in words buyers already use
- [ ] One specific audience named
- [ ] A point of view that implies what not to do
- [ ] Passes the swap test
- [ ] Passes the 15-second clarity test (what, who, cost or next step)
- [ ] Mission, story (under ~250 words), voice do's and don'ts, design basics written down
- [ ] Recognisable assets identified and protected
- [ ] At least one thing in the marketing that couldn't come from a template or an automation
