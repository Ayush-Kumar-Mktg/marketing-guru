# Machine-writing patterns and fixes

Deep reference for the humanize-writing skill. Each pattern has what to look for and how to fix it. The goal is writing a specific person would put their name to.

---

## A. Content patterns

### 1. Inflated importance
**Looks like:** "marks a pivotal moment", "a testament to", "reflects broader trends", "sets the stage for", "an enduring legacy".
**Fix:** State what happened. Let the reader decide whether it matters.
- Before: The redesign marks a pivotal moment in the brand's evolution.
- After: The redesign cut the homepage from nine sections to four.

### 2. Name-dropping coverage
**Looks like:** a list of outlets or follower counts offered as proof of importance.
**Fix:** Cite one specific thing that was said, where and when.

### 3. Fake-depth "-ing" endings
**Looks like:** "...showcasing their commitment to quality", "...reflecting strong demand", "...highlighting the value of clean data".
**Fix:** End the sentence at the fact. If the interpretation matters, give it its own sentence with evidence.

### 4. Brochure language
**Looks like:** nestled, vibrant, stunning, breathtaking, boasts, rich heritage, world-class, must-try.
**Fix:** Replace with a concrete detail. "Known for its Sunday market and a 200-year-old banyan tree" beats "a vibrant town with rich heritage".

### 5. Vague authorities
**Looks like:** "experts say", "studies show", "industry observers note", "many believe".
**Fix:** Name the source, or delete the claim.

### 6. Formula sections
**Looks like:** "Despite these challenges...", "Future outlook", "Challenges and opportunities".
**Fix:** Give specific facts about specific problems, or cut the section.

## B. Language patterns

### 7. Overused machine vocabulary
Words that appear far more in generated text than in human writing. Swap for plain words or cut:

| Avoid | Try instead |
|---|---|
| delve into | look at, dig into |
| leverage (verb) | use |
| robust | strong, reliable (or say how) |
| streamline | simplify, cut steps |
| comprehensive | full, complete (or say what's covered) |
| crucial, pivotal, paramount, vital | important (or show why) |
| foster | build, encourage |
| facilitate | help, make possible |
| transformative, groundbreaking, innovative | say what changed |
| underscore, highlight (verb) | show |
| showcase | show |
| tapestry, landscape (abstract) | name the actual thing |
| testament to | proof that (or cut) |
| seamless | say what's easy |
| unlock | get, open |
| elevate | improve (say how) |
| navigate (abstract) | handle, deal with |
| in today's fast-paced world | cut |
| additionally, furthermore, moreover | and, also, or start the next sentence |

### 8. Avoiding plain "is" and "has"
**Looks like:** "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "boasts", "features", "offers".
**Fix:** "The tool is a scheduling assistant." "The plan has three tiers."

### 9. "It's not X, it's Y" and tag-on negations
**Looks like:** "It's not just about speed, it's about trust." "Not only... but also..." "Set up in minutes, no code needed."
**Fix:** Say the positive claim directly. Turn fragments into real clauses: "Setup takes two minutes and doesn't need a developer."

### 10. Forced groups of three
**Looks like:** "faster, smarter and more reliable", "innovation, inspiration and insight".
**Fix:** Keep only the items that are real and relevant. Two is fine. One is often better.

### 11. Synonym cycling
**Looks like:** "the product... the solution... the platform... the tool" for the same thing in one paragraph.
**Fix:** Pick one word and repeat it. Readers don't mind repetition. They mind confusion.

### 12. False ranges
**Looks like:** "from startups to enterprises", "from the first click to the final sale" where the ends aren't a real scale.
**Fix:** Say who or what specifically.

### 13. Hidden actors
**Looks like:** "Mistakes were made." "No setup required." "Results are delivered automatically."
**Fix:** Name who does what. "You don't need to set anything up." "The app sends results every Monday."

## C. Style patterns

### 14. Dashes everywhere
Long dashes used as all-purpose punctuation are one of the clearest machine tells. Replace with a comma, a full stop, a colon after a complete sentence, or brackets. Many brand style guides (and many editors) now ban them outright.

### 15. Semicolons and mid-sentence colons
Rare in everyday professional writing. Split into two sentences. A colon should follow a complete sentence.

### 16. Decorative bold
Bold every few words and nothing stands out. Bold only what a skimming reader truly needs to find.

### 17. Bold-label bullet lists
**Looks like:**
- **Speed:** It's faster.
- **Quality:** It's better.

**Fix:** Write it as a sentence, or use plain bullets.

### 18. Title Case Headings
Use sentence case: "How we cut churn in half", not "How We Cut Churn In Half".

### 19. Emoji decoration
No emojis on headings or bullets unless the brand voice and channel truly call for them.

### 20. Curly quotes pasted into code or plain text
Use straight quotes in plain-text and technical contexts.

### 21. Even rhythm
Every sentence 15 to 20 words, every paragraph three sentences. Real writing varies. Mix a four-word sentence with a long one that takes its time. Break the pattern on purpose.

### 22. Over-hyphenation
Perfectly consistent hyphenation of common pairs (high-quality, data-driven, end-to-end, decision-making) in every instance reads as machine-made. Hyphenate when it prevents confusion, not by reflex.

## D. Conversation residue

### 23. Chatbot openers and closers
**Looks like:** "Great question!", "Certainly!", "Absolutely!", "I hope this helps", "Let me know if you'd like more", "Here's a...".
**Fix:** Start with the answer. End when the content ends.

### 24. Knowledge-cutoff hedges
**Looks like:** "As of my last update", "based on available information", "while details are limited".
**Fix:** Say what you know, or say plainly that you don't know.

### 25. Fawning tone
Agreeing and praising before every point. Cut it.

### 26. Unprompted both-sides framing
"On one hand... on the other hand..." when nobody asked for a balanced view. Marketing copy takes a position.

### 27. Listing when one answer would do
Five options when the reader needed one recommendation. Recommend, then mention alternatives only if they matter.

## E. Filler and hedging

### 28. Filler phrases
| Wordy | Plain |
|---|---|
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| at this point in time | now |
| has the ability to | can |
| in the event that | if |
| it is important to note that | (cut) |
| it is worth mentioning | (cut) |
| keep in mind that | (cut, just say it) |
| the fact of the matter is | (cut) |

### 29. Stacked hedges
"It could potentially possibly be argued..." becomes "This may affect...". One hedge per claim, only when uncertainty is real.

### 30. Authority theatre
"At its core", "the real question is", "what really matters is", "fundamentally". These promise a deeper truth and then restate the obvious. Cut them and make the point.

### 31. Signposting
"Let's dive in", "let's break this down", "here's what you need to know", "without further ado". Do the thing instead of announcing it.

### 32. Section summaries and generic endings
"In summary...", "Overall...", "The future looks bright", "Exciting times ahead". End on something specific: a number, a next step, a sharp line. Or just stop.

---

## F. Adding a pulse

Removing tells isn't enough. Clean but voiceless writing is just as obvious.

- **Have a view.** React to facts, don't only report them.
- **Be specific about feelings and scenes.** "Refreshing the dashboard at 11pm to see if anyone signed up" beats "launch anxiety".
- **Use "I" and "you"** where the format allows.
- **Allow some mess.** An aside, a self-correction, a short fragment that's the whole thought.
- **Anchor every abstract claim** in a number, a name, a time or a scene.
- **Read it aloud.** If it sounds like a robot reading a list, rewrite it.
- **Match a real voice.** If the user provides a sample of their writing, copy its sentence length, word choice, punctuation habits and how they open paragraphs. Don't "upgrade" their plain words.
