# Growth strategy playbook

Deep reference for the growth-strategy skill.

---

## 1. Growth is a system of five parts

Growth doesn't come from product alone or distribution alone. It comes from five parts lining up:

1. **Market:** who you serve
2. **Product:** what you offer them
3. **Model:** how you make money (pricing, packaging, sales motion)
4. **Brand:** your story and message
5. **Channel:** how you reach the market

Product-market fit is only one pairing out of the ten possible pairings between these five. The traction phase tests very few of them. Growth mode tests all of them at once.

### Why channels "stop working" when you try to scale
Early users find you through your network, communities and word of mouth. They're forgiving, they understand the product despite rough edges, and they tell your story for you. That hides weak foundations: a vague story, a clunky value prop, a placeholder price.

Then you go to cold audiences who owe you nothing, and nothing works. Founders blame the channel. Usually a foundation was misaligned all along.

Before declaring a channel dead, ask:
- **Product ↔ channel:** is the product built for the mindset people are in on this channel? Low-context channels (a social feed) need low-friction products.
- **Model ↔ channel:** can our economics survive this channel's cost per customer?
- **Brand ↔ channel:** does this channel give us room to tell our story?
- **Market ↔ channel:** can we reach our market here at all?

Different diagnosis, different fix. The channel you abandoned might be your best one once the foundations line up. And alignment isn't done once: markets, products and channels drift, so re-check every quarter.

### Story is often the cheapest lever
Many "channel problems" are story problems. See the email-lifecycle skill (story before channel) for the exercise: what would our best customers miss if we vanished, written in their words?

## 2. Advantages vs flywheels

An advantage helps you move faster for a while. A flywheel gets stronger as you grow. Most companies have an early advantage. Few have a true flywheel, and many find out late.

A real flywheel has three properties:
1. Each new user makes the product more useful for the next.
2. It reinforces itself without proportional extra effort.
3. It gets harder to copy as it grows.

If growth needs the same input every cycle, you have momentum, not a flywheel.

### Four flywheel types

**Network effects (the gold standard).** Each new user directly increases value for existing users: marketplaces, social networks, tools where people interact across accounts. Growth improves retention, not just acquisition, and leaving costs something. Common false positives: collecting lots of data, having several user types, or users being technically able to interact. If a new user doesn't change the experience for others, it isn't a network effect.

**Scale.** Growth lowers your costs in a way that strengthens your promise to customers (lower prices, more investment, outspending rivals on acquisition). Ask: does getting bigger make us better for customers, or only cheaper to run? It only matters if your scale advantage is the largest one in the market.

**Embedded (the most overestimated).** Workflows, data, integrations and habits wrap around the product so leaving gets harder. It feels strong from the inside, but switching tools keep improving and habits are fragile without real value. It mostly helps retention. It's strongest in complex B2B setups with many teams and systems, and it only counts if the embedding deepens with use.

**Brand (the most underrated).** Usage itself changes perception and lowers friction for the next customer:
- **Normalising something awkward or risky.** Each happy customer makes the next person more comfortable (for example, staying in a stranger's home, or seeking help for an embarrassing problem).
- **Owning one association** so completely that people stop comparing ("it just works", "it's the default").
- **Community as the product.** The shared identity, rituals and people are the value. Test: if you removed the community, would the product feel much weaker?

### If you don't have a flywheel
That's fine. Many strong businesses grow linearly. Your options:
1. **Accept it.** Build an efficient, durable business on good fundamentals.
2. **Support it.** Use boosters (below) knowing they fade.
3. **Engineer toward one.** Redesign product loops, shift brand toward behaviour change, add community or interaction where it truly adds value.

The mistake is pretending you have one. That's how teams over-invest and misread signals.

## 3. Boosters

A booster is a temporary jump in growth speed. Useful, but finite. Five kinds, and the flywheel each should try to start:

| Booster | Example shape | Flywheel it should spin up |
|---|---|---|
| **Market** | Sudden demand in your category (news, regulation, a crisis) | Brand: turn spike customers into advocates |
| **Product** | A feature that makes the product clearly better for a while | Product: more users → more data or network value |
| **Model** | A pricing, packaging or sales-motion change (for example self-serve where rivals need sales) | Distribution: share gains → pricing power |
| **Brand** | A positioning move that builds lasting preference | Category: brand becomes shorthand for the category, acquisition cost falls |
| **Channel** | Dominating a channel before rivals arrive | Content or audience: reach compounds without matching spend |

Audit each initiative:
1. Booster or flywheel?
2. If booster, which flywheel is it spinning up?
3. If none, what's the plan when it fades?

Teams who can't answer the third question are on a treadmill, always hunting the next booster.

Breakout companies pull away when their flywheel spins fast enough that catching up takes more than matching their spend or features.

## 4. Audience first, product second

As building gets cheaper, distribution becomes the scarce asset. One low-risk path:
1. Build an audience around expertise you have (content, community, newsletter).
2. Focus on one platform where that audience already is.
3. Launch small products for them, learning what they need.
4. Keep selling new products into the growing audience.

You don't need millions of followers. A few hundred to a thousand of the right people (a newsletter read by 500 buyers in your niche) beats a big mixed audience.

### Pre-sell instead of asking for feedback
Feedback is free, and people are generous with compliments that cost nothing. Money is honest. Validate with deposits or full pre-orders:
- A landing page with a clear value prop, proof and a realistic product mock-up
- Early-access pricing with a meaningful deposit
- A specific threshold ("we build it if 50 people pre-order")
- For enterprise: paid pilots or design partnerships

### Manual before mechanical
No audience yet? Sell a service version you deliver by hand, learn and get paid, then productise. Watch for the service trap: set a clear trigger for moving to product (a revenue or client number), cap intake or hire help, or you'll be too busy delivering to ever build.

## 5. Pivots under pressure

When a wave threatens your core product:
1. **Don't build a taller sandcastle.** Out-featuring a platform shift rarely works. Move while you still have cash and credibility.
2. **Niche is the moat.** Build for specific groups in depth rather than everyone a little. "Watch bands" is generic. "Rubber watch bands that don't scratch your laptop" is defensible.
3. **Bet on what you already do best.** In a downturn, reallocate to experiments that reuse core strengths, rather than only cutting.

## 6. Product-led growth and pricing

### PLG is a spectrum
1. **Free trial only:** time-limited. Closer to a product-gated demo than true PLG.
2. **Freemium with limits:** use forever, hit feature or usage limits. Upgrade when the limit hurts.
3. **Freemium with network value:** the free tier gets better as more people use it. Upgrades often come from team adoption.
4. **Free core, paid infrastructure:** the product is free and money comes from consumption at scale (storage, usage, seats).

Most PLG advice assumes stage 2. Know which stage you're in.

### Pricing tiers need triggers
Don't default to three tiers by habit. For each tier: which segment is it for, and what specific event moves them up? If you can't name the moment someone upgrades, the pricing isn't doing its job.

### Choosing what to charge for
1. Does it grow as the customer gets more value?
2. Can the customer understand and predict it?
3. Can they control it (paying for outcomes, not accidents)?
4. Does it encourage the usage that delivers the most value?

Most companies pick what's easiest to count. Usage-based pricing lowers the entry commitment and grows naturally with value, but revenue is less predictable and heavy users can feel punished. It works when the metric tracks value closely. For AI products, pricing on outcomes (what the AI helped accomplish) aligns better than pricing on inputs.

### Activation drives everything
- Activation is the moment a user has used the product enough to form an informed opinion. Users who activate are far more likely to pay. Users who don't will churn whatever the price.
- Pick a north star in the middle of the funnel (activated users, weekly active paying users), not signups, which are easy to inflate with poor-fit users.
- See the conversion-cro skill for onboarding design.

### When product hands off to sales
Pure self-serve breaks when deals get large or need procurement. Use **product-qualified leads**: users whose behaviour shows a sales conversation would be welcome. Good signals:
- reached the activation milestone
- invited teammates
- hit a usage ceiling an upgrade would solve
- changed role or company (a new buying context)

Sales in a product-led company unlocks expansion in accounts already using the product. It doesn't chase cold leads.

## 7. B2B: signal-led go-to-market

### Score accounts on three layers
1. **Profile:** does the company fit (industry, size, tech stack)?
2. **Opportunity:** are there signs of need or budget?
3. **Signal:** is someone acting like a buyer right now?

Profile says who could buy. Signals say who is buying. Weight the last two heavily.

### Signal sources
- Third-party intent (who is researching your category)
- First-party engagement (content, webinars, pricing page visits)
- CRM changes (stalled deals reviving, champions changing jobs)
- Job postings for roles that point to the problem you solve
- News and funding events

The job of a signal system is to turn these into a ranked account list with a recommended next action.

### A warm-up path before sales
Before routing an account to a rep, run it through a personalised sequence: content matched to its signals, ads mirroring what it's researching, proof from similar companies and a clear conversion moment (demo, trial, event). Only accounts that progress get a rep's time.

### Audience-first paid
Build the list of accounts and people you want first. Enrich it. Load it to every channel with a consistent message. Feed every interaction back into the CRM at account level. Boost organic posts that already resonate with that audience. Paid becomes a targeted account program with a budget.

### Build systems, not campaigns
A campaign ends. A system compounds. Measure pipeline, revenue influence and account progress, not activity (impressions, opens, raw lead counts). Design the system first (which data feeds which process, which signal triggers which action), then pick tools.

## 8. Growth strategy checklist

- [ ] All five parts (market, product, model, brand, channel) checked for alignment
- [ ] Channel "failures" re-diagnosed by pairing before being dropped
- [ ] Each initiative labelled booster or flywheel, with the flywheel it feeds
- [ ] Honest answer on whether a flywheel exists
- [ ] Validation by money (pre-sales, pilots), not compliments
- [ ] Pricing tiers each have a named upgrade trigger
- [ ] North star sits at activation, not signups
- [ ] Metrics track business outcomes, not activity
