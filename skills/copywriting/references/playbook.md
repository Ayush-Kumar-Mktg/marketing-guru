# Copywriting and messaging

Use this file for headlines, value props, elevator pitches, taglines, product descriptions, ad copy angles and any time copy "sounds fine but does nothing".

---

## 1. The first question every line must answer

A stranger reading your copy is asking one thing: what do I get? Most weak copy answers a different question, usually "what did we build?" or "what are we proud of?"

Spot it fast. Weak lines tend to be:

- **Self-centred.** They talk about the company, the team, the tech, the origin story.
- **Abstract.** "Solutions", "platform", "innovation", "best-in-class". Nothing you can picture.
- **Flat.** Accurate, polite and forgettable.

Flip it by moving the camera from the product to the customer:

| Inside-out (weak) | Outside-in (strong) |
|---|---|
| AI-powered expense categorisation engine | Close your books on the 1st, not the 15th |
| 500+ verified home cleaners | Come home to a clean flat without spending your Sunday on it |
| Triple-filtered cold-pressed juice | The 4pm slump, gone before your next meeting |

How to do the flip, step by step:

1. Write the weak line exactly as the team would say it.
2. Ask "so what?" until you hit a feeling or a result the customer cares about.
3. Write four quick versions of that result, one each for:
   - the plain benefit (what they get)
   - the frustration they feel today (in their words)
   - a small scene where the problem goes away
   - the before/after change in their life or work
4. Pick the one you would say out loud to a friend. Cut it in half.

Small rules that do a lot of work:

- Talk to one person. "You" beats "teams" and "users".
- Lead with the "after" state, then explain the "how" later.
- Verbs beat nouns. Get, stop, skip, wake up, finish, keep.
- Use contractions and normal words. If nobody says it in a meeting, don't write it.

## 2. Specificity is the cheapest persuasion there is

Vague claims trigger the reader's "marketing filter". Specific ones get past it, because specific sounds like evidence.

- "Fast" → "Loads before you finish blinking (0.4s on 4G)"
- "Loved by customers" → "4.8 stars from 11,200 reviews"
- "Saves time" → "Cuts weekly reporting from 3 hours to 20 minutes"

Odd, exact numbers feel more true than round ones. 47 beats 50. "19 tries" beats "many tries". Never invent them though. A fake number that gets caught costs more than a vague claim ever would.

People also respond to one person more than to a crowd. One named customer story will usually beat a statistic about thousands.

## 3. Finding the value prop: the "bad alternative" method

People don't compare you to the best product in your category. They compare you to whatever they do today, which is usually a spreadsheet, a WhatsApp group, an intern, a workaround or nothing at all.

Three steps:

1. **Name the bad alternative.** What do people do when your product doesn't exist?
2. **Name why you are better than that alternative.** Be concrete.
3. **Turn it into an instruction.** That instruction is your value prop.

Example (an invoicing tool for freelancers):

1. Bad alternative: chasing clients on email and tracking payments in a sheet.
2. Better: invoices go out on a schedule and nudge late payers automatically.
3. Value prop: "Get paid on time without sending a single awkward reminder."

Run a quick diagnosis by asking ten recent customers: "What would you have done if you hadn't found us?"

- Mostly "a competitor" → your fight is **differentiation**.
- Mostly "kept doing it manually" → your fight is **conviction**. Show why change is worth the effort.
- Mostly "nothing, we weren't ready" → your fight is **timing**. You need demand creation, not conversion tweaks.

## 4. The swap test

Take your homepage hero. Replace your logo and name with a competitor's. Does it still make sense?

If yes, you have no positioning. "Powerful, flexible, easy to use" fits every product ever made. Rewrite until the page would be a lie on a competitor's site.

A good value prop is:

- **Specific.** Numbers, situations, people.
- **Believable.** You can back it up.
- **Relevant.** It hits a problem the buyer already ranks near the top.

Positioning sentence to draft from (write three versions, then test):

> For [specific person] who [specific problem], [product] is the [category] that [specific outcome], because [proof nobody else can claim].

## 5. Headlines

The headline does most of the work. Far more people read it than read anything else, so spend your time there.

A strong headline is at least one of:

- **Useful** (it promises a clear gain)
- **Ultra-specific** (numbers, names, situations)
- **Uncopyable** (only you could say it)
- **Urgent** (a real reason to act now, never a fake one)

### Building a hero headline in three layers

1. **Value.** Your sharpest outcome, from the bad-alternative exercise.
2. **Hook.** Add one of two things:
   - a **bold, specific claim** that makes people think "wait, how?"
   - a line that **kills the biggest objection** before it forms ("no code", "no contract", "set up in 10 minutes")
3. **Audience.** Make the right person feel this was written for them. The narrower the audience, the sharper the words can be.

Find objections by asking customers two questions:

- "What almost stopped you from buying?"
- "Why do you think other people haven't bought yet?"

Handle the biggest one in the hero. Handle the rest further down the page. Don't cram.

Watch the BS meter. A claim that sounds too good to be true makes the whole page feel like a scam.

### Subheadline

The headline sells the feeling. The subheadline supplies the logic. Use it to answer one of:

- What exactly is this?
- How is the headline's claim possible?
- Who is it for?

One sentence is fine. Two is the limit. A subheadline that restates the headline in different words is wasted space.

### CTA

The button should feel like the next step of the story you started, and say what happens on click.

- Weak: "Get started", "Submit", "Learn more"
- Strong: "Start my free trial (no card)", "See my savings", "Book a 20-minute demo"

If the product is hard to grasp in one screen, the first CTA can simply scroll people down to the explanation.

## 6. Pattern interrupts: earning the first second

People see thousands of marketing messages a day and ignore almost all of them on autopilot. A pattern interrupt is anything that breaks that autopilot for a moment and makes someone think "wait, what?"

Differentiation is choosing a different lane. A pattern interrupt is the moment that makes people look over. You want both.

Headline moves that interrupt:

- **Apparent contradiction.** "We grew sign-ups by deleting our pricing page."
- **Strange specificity.** "Tested on 212 picky toddlers. 197 asked for more."
- **Deadpan honesty.** "Not the cheapest. Definitely the least annoying."
- **Reverse logic.** "The best meeting is the one you cancel."
- **Curiosity gap.** "We launched this as a joke. It now pays our rent."

Content hook types:

- **Against the grain.** Challenge a rule your audience treats as gospel.
- **Confession.** Admit a mistake, with a number attached.
- **Mini-drama.** Two sentences of tension: it was going well, then something broke.
- **Win that shouldn't have worked.** Make people question their playbook.
- **Anti-hype.** Puncture a trend everyone is excited about.
- **Visual jolt.** In video, the first frame should be strange, moving or unexpected.

Rules: open a loop the reader needs to close, make the stakes obvious, and pay the hook off. A hook that doesn't deliver burns trust.

### Cold email version

Dead-on-arrival subject lines all follow the sender's agenda: "Quick question", "Following up", "Partnership opportunity", "Are you the right person?".

Subject lines that get opened sound like a human, create curiosity or admit what they are:

- "Yes, this is a cold email. It's a short one."
- "This might be a terrible idea for you"
- "Noticed something on your pricing page"

Inside the email: readable in 15 seconds, first line conversational, short blocks, honest sign-off ("A polite no would still make my day"). A quick "forgot to attach this" follow-up ten minutes later often outperforms a "just following up" three days later.

## 7. Don't make one asset do every job

Funnels break when the first touch tries to tell the whole story. Buyers move through small beliefs, one at a time:

1. "That's my problem."
2. "This looks made for people like me."
3. "They seem credible."
4. "This is different from what I've tried."
5. "This feels safe to try."

**One asset, one belief, one action.** Before you publish anything, finish this sentence: "The single belief this asset creates is ______." If you can't, it's doing too much.

A clean sequence:

| Stage | Reader's head | Job of the message |
|---|---|---|
| First touch (ad, post) | "I have this pain, who are you?" | Name the pain or tease the outcome |
| Landing hero | "Is this for me?" | "We fix this, for people like you" |
| Mid-page | "How is this different?" | Mechanism and proof |
| CTA area | "I don't want to regret this" | Remove risk, make the next step tiny |
| After purchase | "Was it worth it?" | Pay off the original promise with evidence |

Three common failures: a pitch deck on the homepage, a feature list in a cold ad, and a hard sell to people who just met you.

## 8. The 30-second pitch

Everyone on the team should tell roughly the same story. Ask three colleagues to explain the company in 30 seconds and compare. Different stories mean diluted positioning.

Structure:

1. **What you are.** One plain sentence, the way an encyclopedia would describe you.
2. **The problem.** Two or three sentences about the pain your buyer feels. Nobody wakes up wanting software. They wake up annoyed about something.
3. **Why you.** One sharp claim only you can make. Good differentiators: faster or cheaper with proof, access others don't have, a completely different method, a deliberate trade-off your audience values, or owning a niche completely.
4. **Ask.** End with a clear next step, then stop talking and listen.

Avoid "we have a great team" and "we care about customers". Everyone says that.

## 9. Frameworks to draft with

Use these to get a first draft out, then edit hard.

- **Before / After / Bridge.** Life now, life once the problem is gone, your product as the bridge.
- **Problem / Agitate / Solve.** Name the problem, make the cost of ignoring it vivid, then offer the fix.
- **Attention / Interest / Desire / Action.** Stop them, make them curious, make them want it, tell them exactly what to do.
- **Feature → Benefit → Value.** For each feature, write what it does for the user, then why that matters in their actual day.
  - Wide-mouth bottle → fills faster → less time at the cooler, more time on the trail.

## 10. Social proof that persuades

- **Mine reviews for language.** Read reviews of your product and your competitors on marketplaces, forums and app stores. Log three things: memorable phrases, what people want, what they react to emotionally. Steal their words, not your team's.
- **Skeptic-first testimonials.** Ask customers: what nearly stopped you? What happened after? What one feature do you use most? Would you recommend it, and why? Testimonials that start with doubt read as more honest.
- **Put proof next to the claim it supports.** A testimonial about saved time belongs beside the time-saving claim, not in a wall of logos at the bottom.
- **Ordinary beats glossy.** Real customers, real faces and real language usually beat polished models.
- **Negative reviews can work** when you're confident enough to make fun of them.

---

## 11. Voice, humour and emotion

- People decide emotionally and justify with logic. Lead with the feeling, support with facts.
- Stories beat information. Give the reader a hero (them), a villain (the problem) and a small, concrete scene.
- Sensory words help when you sell something physical: what it looks, feels, smells or sounds like.
- Humour builds trust when it fits the brand and the audience, never at the cost of clarity, and only alongside real credibility. Write down "our brand always sounds like..." and "our brand never sounds like..." before trying to be funny.
- Clear first, human second. When copy gets wordy and vague, it's usually because the writer isn't sure what they mean.

---

## 12. Quick copy audit

Run this on any page, ad or email:

- [ ] Does the first line answer "what do I get?"
- [ ] Would it break if a competitor's logo were on it?
- [ ] Is there at least one specific number, name or situation?
- [ ] Is the biggest objection handled early?
- [ ] Does each asset have one job?
- [ ] Does the CTA say what happens next?
- [ ] Would you say this sentence out loud to a customer?
- [ ] Does it contain at least one claim or example a competitor couldn't honestly copy?
