# Marketing psychology: the bias library

Deep reference for the marketing-psychology skill. Each entry has what it is, where it applies, how to use it and where it goes wrong. Ordered by the stage of the buyer's journey where it does the most work.

---

## Stage 1: Perception (first impressions)

### Mere exposure
**What:** People prefer what feels familiar, even when they can't remember where they saw it. Familiar feels safe.
**Use:**
- Consistent colours, layouts, phrases and formats so you're recognisable without your name.
- Repeat formats (a numbered newsletter subject, a weekly series name) so loyal readers spot you instantly.
- Retargeting, content, podcasts, guest posts and PR all add exposure.
- Remind people before something goes out (the day before and a few hours before).
**Misuse:** Exposure without value becomes annoyance. Frequency caps exist for a reason.

### Space-to-product ratio
**What:** Items shown with more space around them feel more premium. Crowded displays read as discount.
**Use:** White space in product photos, fewer items per page, calm layouts. It helps new brands most, since established premium brands already have the perception.
**Misuse:** Minimalism that removes the information people need. Don't trade clarity for vibes.

### Processing fluency
**What:** Things that are easy to read and understand feel more true, more trustworthy and worth more. Effort triggers second-guessing.
**Use:**
- Short navigation (group links if you need more than about seven).
- Plain words a 13-year-old understands. Short sentences.
- Fewer form fields: one name field, billing equals shipping by default, optional fields behind a link, city filled from postcode.
- Account creation at the end of checkout, not the start.
- Pop-ups spaced out and triggered by real engagement.
**Misuse:** Oversimplifying something complex until it's misleading.

### Framing
**What:** The same fact lands differently depending on how it's presented. "Kills 98% of germs" beats "2% of germs survive."
**Use:**
- **Gain framing** for products about progress and growth ("get instant relief").
- **Loss framing** for products that prevent a problem ("avoid the 3am wake-up").
- Price framing: per day vs per year, savings vs cost, "less than your daily chai."
- Test both. Results often surprise.
**Misuse:** Framing that changes the facts rather than the presentation.

### Noble edge
**What:** People prefer companies that act responsibly.
**Use only if real:** tie your actual cause to your mission and story, publish impact with exact numbers, offer sustainable options in checkout, pursue credible certifications.
**Misuse:** Performative support with no action. Audiences check, and the backlash is worse than silence.

## Stage 2: Decision

### Social proof
**What:** When unsure, people copy people like them.
**Use:** Specific numbers ("2,847 teams signed up this week"), segment-specific proof ("used by 200+ clinics"), reviews near the CTA, real faces, comparison reviews ("better than what I used before").
**Misuse:** Fake counts, fake reviews, or proof from people unlike the buyer.

### Authority
**What:** People trust credentials, experts and data.
**Use:** Expert endorsements in categories where quality needs expertise (finance, health, software), data from your own experiments, track record numbers.
**Misuse:** Borrowed authority you don't have permission for, or credentials unrelated to the claim.

### Scarcity and urgency
**What:** Limited things feel worth more. Deadlines force decisions.
**Use:** Real stock limits, real cohort sizes, real end dates, placed next to the CTA.
**Misuse:** Fake countdown timers and "only 2 left" that resets on refresh. Stacking every urgency trick on one page feels like a pushy salesperson.

### Loss aversion
**What:** Losing something hurts about twice as much as gaining the same thing feels good.
**Use:** "Don't lose your progress", "your saved items will expire", common-mistakes content, trial endings that show what they'll lose.
**Misuse:** Manufactured fear about problems the product doesn't solve.

### Anchoring
**What:** The first number people see sets the reference point for everything after.
**Use:** Show the higher plan or the cost of the problem first. Show the original price next to the offer. Compare to an expensive alternative (a consultant, an agency, an hour of a senior person's time).
**Misuse:** Invented "original prices" nobody ever paid.

### Decoy effect
**What:** Adding a third option that's clearly worse than one of the others makes that one look like the obvious pick (a medium that costs almost as much as the large).
**Use:** Decide which plan you want most people on. Price a decoy close to it that offers some but not all of its value. Make differences obvious in a comparison table and visually mark the target plan.
**Misuse:** A decoy that's a rip-off. It should still be a fair option for someone.

### Default effect
**What:** Most people keep whatever is pre-selected.
**Use:** Pre-select the most common sensible choice (plan, frequency, settings). Make changing it easy and quiet. If most people change a default, it's the wrong default.
**Misuse:** Pre-ticked add-ons or consent people wouldn't choose.

### Choice overload
**What:** Too many options cause stalling or no decision.
**Use:** Fewer plans, a recommended option, filters, a quiz that narrows choices, one CTA per section.
**Misuse:** Hiding options people need.

### Endowment effect
**What:** People value things more once they feel like theirs.
**Use:** Free trials with real data in them, try-on and place-in-your-room tools, "your cart misses you" language, "your 3,000 points are waiting".
**Misuse:** Implying ownership of rewards people haven't earned, in a way that feels like a trick.

### Goal gradient
**What:** People speed up as a finish line gets closer.
**Use:** Progress bars and numbered steps in signup and checkout. Copy like "halfway there", "last step". Start loyalty cards partly filled.
**Misuse:** Fake progress that jumps back.

### Reciprocity
**What:** People feel obliged to return a favour.
**Use:** Give real value before asking: a useful tool, a template, a genuinely helpful audit. A surprise bonus after purchase.
**Misuse:** "Gifts" that are really bait with strings attached.

### Commitment and consistency
**What:** After a small yes, people tend to stay consistent with it.
**Use:** Micro-commitments (a one-question quiz, "reveal your price" button, a free tier) before the big ask.
**Misuse:** Foot-in-the-door chains that escalate into something they never wanted.

### Zero price effect
**What:** "Free" pulls far harder than a very low price.
**Use:** Free shipping thresholds, free first month, a free tool or tier as an entry point.
**Misuse:** "Free" with hidden costs revealed later.

### Pratfall effect
**What:** A small, honest flaw makes a strong brand more likeable and believable.
**Use:** Admit a real limitation ("not the cheapest, definitely the least annoying"), show a few honest 3 and 4-star reviews.
**Misuse:** Admitting flaws that matter for the buyer's decision as a joke.

## Stage 3: Product experience and retention

### Labour illusion
**What:** Showing the work behind a result makes people value it more and wait more patiently.
**Use:** Progress indicators with descriptive copy ("checking 4 million listings for your dates..."). Even instant results can feel worth more when the work is visible.
**Misuse:** Fake delays to look busy when the user is in a hurry.

### IKEA effect
**What:** People value what they helped build.
**Use:** Customisation, templates as starting points, clear step-by-step instructions, contests and galleries where customers share what they made.
**Misuse:** DIY that's too hard. Failure makes people feel stupid and blame the product.

### Peak-end rule
**What:** People judge an experience by its emotional peak and how it ended, not the average.
**Use:** A surprise gift, a handwritten note, praise after a task, a delightful unboxing, frictionless returns, warm confirmation pages, fast compassionate support. Design every ending.
**Misuse:** A great ending covering up a bad core experience. It only works short term.

### Variable rewards
**What:** Unpredictable rewards hold attention longer than predictable ones.
**Use:** Random bonus gifts in subscriptions, surprise upgrades, occasional giveaways for loyal customers, mystery items.
**Misuse:** Gambling-like mechanics that exploit compulsion, especially with vulnerable users or children.

### Curiosity gap and mystery
**What:** An open question pulls people forward. A mystery reward often beats a known one of the same value.
**Use:** Hooks that open a loop, "reveal" buttons, mystery bonuses.
**Misuse:** Clickbait that never pays off.

## Small levers worth testing

- **Gaze direction:** a face looking toward the CTA pulls the viewer's eyes there too.
- **Odd beats logical:** test at least one variation that makes no rational sense. The strange option often wins.
- **Specific numbers feel true:** 47 beats 50 (but only if it's real).
- **Single hero:** one person's story moves people more than a statistic about thousands.

---

## Ethics line

Ask before using any lever: would the customer feel good about this decision if they saw exactly how we influenced it? If not, don't ship it. Persuasion makes a true, useful offer easier to accept. Manipulation makes people act against their own interest. The second one also destroys trust, reviews and retention.
