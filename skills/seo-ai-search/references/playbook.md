# SEO and AI search playbook

Deep reference for the seo-ai-search skill. Part A covers classic search (getting pages to rank and convert). Part B covers AI answer engines (getting recommended when someone asks a chatbot). Part C covers measurement.

---

# Part A: Content-led SEO

## A1. How ranking works now, in three layers

1. **Can the search engine reach and read you?** Technical health: crawlable pages, a sitemap, sensible robots rules, fast load, no broken links, HTTPS, a clear site structure. If this fails, nothing else matters.
2. **Does it believe you know this topic?** Topical authority: how strongly your site is tied to a subject. You build it with depth of content on that subject and with links from relevant sites.
3. **Do people stay satisfied?** The strongest signal is whether the searcher has to go back and search again. A page that ends the search keeps its spot. A page that sends people back to the results loses it.

The practical result: generic "SEO content" no longer holds top positions. You have to please the algorithm to get a trial ranking, then please the reader to keep it.

## A2. Three myths worth killing early

- **"More search volume, more customers."** High-volume, top-of-funnel terms bring readers who rarely buy. Low-volume terms close to a purchase usually bring more revenue.
- **"Bigger budget wins."** Publishing more thin pages worked when links ruled. Relevance and quality rule now, so a small team writing fewer, better pages can win.
- **"We'll never beat the big domains."** Many current rankings are leftovers from older tactics. You can win on content depth and page experience, where incumbents are often lazy.

## A3. Three shifts to internalise

1. **Go narrow.** Own the topics your product is about, from every angle and intent, before going broad.
2. **Topics replace single keywords.** One page per search intent, covering the related phrasings and sub-questions inside it.
3. **Context decides.** The page format has to match what searchers expect: a tool, a list, a guide, a video, a template.

## A4. The eight-step process

### Step 1: Fix major technical issues
Small sites on modern site builders usually only need a check. Large sites and programmatic sites (thousands of generated pages) need real technical work. Check:
- **Access:** sitemap, robots rules, internal links, a logical folder structure
- **Understanding:** title tags, headings, structured data (schema)
- **Serving:** mobile layout, no intrusive pop-ups, speed, HTTPS, no broken links or redirect chains, healthy page-experience scores

### Step 2: Build a keyword list, bottom-up
Two ways to research keywords:
- **Top-down (volume play):** start with broad awareness terms and work down. Lots of traffic, little revenue, slow.
- **Bottom-up (pain-point play):** start with the problems and questions your buyers have, then find the searches that match. Lower volume, much higher intent. Start here, especially in B2B.

Sources, in order of value:
1. **What already works.** In Google Search Console, export the last three months of queries. Topics where you already get impressions are the easiest to grow.
2. **Customer conversations.** Ask customers:
   - What problem were you trying to solve before you found us?
   - What would you use if we disappeared?
   - How would you describe us to a friend?
   - What are the top three things you get from us?
   - What would you type into a search bar to find something like us?
3. **Your own team.** Support knows the top questions. Sales knows what prospects compare you to and why deals close. Product knows the most used features. The questions they answer every week are your best articles.
4. **Communities.** Forum and Reddit threads that rank for a question are a sign of demand with weak competition. If the best answer on page one is a forum thread, a dedicated page can win.
5. **Search features.** Autocomplete, "People also ask" and related searches reveal phrasing you'd never guess.
6. **Brainstorm.** Product terms ("[category] software", "best [category] for [audience]") plus value-prop terms ("how to [job your product does]").
7. **Competitors.** Run their pages through any keyword tool to see what they rank for.

Before creating any page, apply one test: is there evidence people search for this, and does our product answer what they are trying to do? If either answer is no, skip it.

### Step 3: Group and prioritise
- Group phrasings with the same intent into one cluster. One cluster, one page.
- The most-searched phrase is the primary keyword. The rest are secondary.
- Prioritise by intent first, volume second. A term that signals buying beats a bigger term that signals curiosity.

### Step 4: Read the results page before writing
Search the keyword and study the top 10 to 30 results. You're looking for two kinds of pattern:
- **Hard requirements:** the page type every result shares, and the level of authority needed. If every result is a broad guide from a big domain with thousands of pages on the topic, a new site won't break in yet.
- **Soft patterns:** common elements that help but aren't mandatory, like title phrasing, video, reviews, a calculator, original data, an angle.

Questions to answer:
- Which page type dominates?
- What is the main intent?
- What media and trust signals appear?
- What angle does every result take, and could a different one win?

### Step 5: Pick the right page type
For most B2B and SaaS sites, almost every keyword fits one of four types. Build in this order:

1. **Product and use-case pages** (bottom of funnel, highest priority). Terms like "[use case] software", "[industry] software", "best [use case] tool for [industry]". If the product will only ever do one thing, the homepage can target it. If it does many things, give each use case its own page. Include the main features, clear demo or pricing CTA, customer proof and a detailed FAQ.
2. **Comparison and "best of" pages** (middle of funnel). Terms like "[competitor] alternatives", "[a] vs [b]", "best [category] for [size or integration]". These searchers already want a product like yours. Put yourself first and say why, honestly. Be specific about where you win and say who you're not right for. Use original screenshots and real opinions from someone who has used the tools. Write for an informed reader. Keep the tone fair to competitors.
3. **Resource articles** (top of funnel). How-to, what-is, templates, examples, checklists. They convert less, so build them after the bottom-funnel pages. They earn their place by supporting the bigger cluster pages.
4. **Topic hubs** (top of funnel). A broad guide for a big category term that covers each sub-question briefly and links out to a dedicated page for each. This keeps people on your site and tells search engines the whole topic lives with you.

### Step 6: Write something original
Most ranking content is a summary of other ranking content. To beat it:
1. **Cover the basics the results page demands.** If every top result has a "best practices" and an "examples" section, so should you. Quality is relative to what's ranking.
2. **Add specificity.** Real details, numbers, edge cases and product nuance that only someone who has done the work would know.
3. **Add at least one original element,** from least to most effort:
   - a contrarian view, backed with evidence (not clickbait)
   - your own first-person experience and opinion
   - an interview with a practitioner or expert
   - original research or data you collected

### Step 7: Make the page a pleasure to use
- A table of contents with jump links on long pages
- An author with a real bio
- Short paragraphs, clear headings, a narrow reading column, a comfortable font size
- Original images and diagrams over stock photos
- A short video near the top if the topic suits it
- Interactive pieces where they help: calculators, quizzes, templates. Tools are hard to copy and attract links naturally.

### Step 8: Link internally with intent
Backlinks bring authority into your site. Internal links carry it to the pages that need it.
- **Structure:** group pages in clear folders by topic (`/topic/subtopic/`). No orphan pages.
- **Hubs and spokes:** the hub links to every spoke from the relevant section, and each spoke links back.
- **Lend authority:** link from your strongest pages (often the homepage) to your most important commercial pages.
- **Anchor text:** describe the destination naturally. Vary the wording. Don't use the same anchor for two different pages.
- **Relevance over placement:** put the link where the reader would want it.
- **Maintenance:** each week, add links from older posts to new ones.

## A5. Maintain and prune

Every few months, audit every URL with: organic sessions, inbound links, publish and update dates, how many keywords rank in the top 3, 10 and 100, impressions, click-through rate and the 12-month trend. Then pick one of four actions:

| Action | When |
|---|---|
| **Update** | Ranks for many terms but not near the top, is stale, has falling traffic, high impressions with low clicks, or is thin |
| **Leave** | Recently published, or performing well enough that changing it is a risk |
| **Delete** | No links, no rankings, time-bound (old promos), thin |
| **Redirect** | Low traffic but has backlinks. Redirect to the most relevant page, never blindly to the homepage |

Check business value by hand before deleting (some pages exist for paid traffic). Prune in batches of about 10% and watch what happens before the next batch. Removing dead weight often lifts the rest of the site, because authority and crawl attention stop leaking into pages nobody needs.

## A6. Product-led SEO

The most durable search assets come from the product itself, not from the content team. Templates, public profiles, listings, user reviews, tool outputs and community answers can each become a page that only you can create, and the library grows as the product grows. Ask: what does our product create that people search for? Then make those pages public and indexable.

Programmatic (product-generated) pages win specific, navigational searches at scale. Editorial pages win expertise and "how to" searches, and earn the authority that helps programmatic pages rank. Do both.

# Part B: AI search and answer engines

## B1. What changed

Chat assistants don't show ten blue links. They give a short list of three to five options with a description of each. If you're not on the list, there's no page two.

Three differences from classic search:
1. **Reputation, not rankings.** The assistant forms an opinion of your brand from everything it has seen: your site, reviews, forums, press, comparison articles, docs, transcripts.
2. **Every assistant sees a different internet.** Different training data, different sources, different update cycles. The sites each one cites overlap far less than you'd expect. Being visible in one says little about the others.
3. **Size matters less.** Assistants reward clarity and consistency. A small brand that is described the same specific way across many niche sources can beat a large brand with a vague, scattered presence.

Assistants learn about you in two ways: what they absorbed during training, and what they fetch from the live web when answering. Plan for both.

## B2. The 20-minute visibility check

1. **Ask what buyers ask.** In each major assistant, run 5 to 10 unbranded questions a buyer would ask:
   - "Best [category] for [audience]"
   - "How do I solve [problem]?"
   - "[Competitor] alternatives"
   - "What tools do [role] use for [job]?"
2. **Test every major assistant** and log results in a grid: query × assistant → present or not, and position.
3. **Read how you're described.** This is the step that teaches the most. Is the description accurate? Specific or generic? Does it mention what makes you different? Compare with competitors' descriptions.
4. **Diagnose each loss.** Usually one of three gaps:
   - **Coverage:** competitors appear across more sources (articles, comparison pages, reviews, forum replies, podcasts). You have a homepage and a blog post.
   - **Consistency:** you tell different stories in different places ("enterprise-grade" on the site, "for solo founders" on a launch page, "cheap" in reviews).
   - **Specificity:** they're positioned for a niche, you're positioned for everyone.
5. **Prioritise by buying intent.** Fix comparison, "best for", "alternatives" and "which should I use" questions first. Then how-to questions. Broad education last.

## B3. Five levers for beating bigger brands

1. **Clarity.** Put the direct answer to the likely question in the first two sentences of the page. Context comes after.
2. **A specific position.** Big brands own the generic category. Own a sub-position ("the [category] for [specific audience]"). Being the obvious answer to a narrow question beats being one of many answers to a broad one.
3. **Off-site presence.** Show up where your category is discussed: communities, review sites, industry newsletters, podcasts, comparison articles. Contribute for real, never spam.
4. **Structure that's easy to extract.** Definitions first, then examples. Headings that match how people phrase questions. Numbered steps. An FAQ at the end of long pages. Structured data for important entities. Restructure existing pages before writing new ones.
5. **Measurement.** Track a fixed set of queries across assistants every week or month.

## B4. Three reasons assistants ignore or misdescribe you

Fix them in this order:
1. **Unclear entity.** If your brand is described with a different tagline, category or audience everywhere, the assistant can't form a clear picture. Write one **brand entity brief**:
   - one-sentence definition of what you do
   - the main category you want to own
   - three to five attributes you want associated with you
   - your ideal customer in specific terms

   Then use that exact language on your About page, review site profiles, company social pages, press releases and speaker bios.
2. **Not answerable.** Most brand pages are written to impress, not to answer. List the 10 questions buyers ask most. Find the page that should answer each one. Rewrite its opening to answer directly, with support below.
3. **Uncredited insights.** Your original idea gets cited via a bigger site that covered it later. Publish with clear author, date and organisation details. Use structured data for authorship. Link back to the original wherever you share the insight. Earn coverage in sources assistants trust.

**Ecosystem rule:** your main claims should appear, in consistent words, in at least five to seven independent places.

## B5. Making it compound

- **Brand-category association.** Content that ties your brand name to category terms, mentions in category discussions, comparison pages that include you, and press in trusted outlets all teach assistants that "[category]" means you.
- **Steady output.** A site with deep, consistent coverage of a topic surfaces more than a site with a few brilliant pieces.
- **Content order:** definitional ("what is [category]") → comparison ("[a] vs [b]") → use case ("how to do [job]") → brand ("about us"). Most teams invert this and over-invest in brand pages.
- **The loop:** pick ~20 target questions → check assistants regularly → log appearances and misdescriptions → when you appear, find which content caused it → make more of that. The fourth step is the one most teams skip, and it's what makes the system compound.
- **Don't game it.** Assistants are built to surface what people trust. Being useful everywhere your audience already is beats tricks.

## B6. Paid placements inside assistants

Ads inside AI assistants are emerging. They're targeted by conversation context rather than keywords, and they sit inside an answer, so they must read as a helpful continuation:
- a specific claim that answers the underlying question
- proof relevant to that context
- a short, clear next step

Build organic presence first, because it's cheaper and makes paid placements more credible. When testing paid: install conversion tracking before spending, run a few context sets from broad to narrow with two ads each, leave it alone for two weeks, then refine based on which conversations you appeared in. Treat paid and organic as one system: paid covers high-intent gaps, organic lowers the cost of paid, and winning paid messages feed back into organic content. Formats will change. The principle won't.

# Part C: Measurement

| What | How often | Tool type |
|---|---|---|
| Keyword positions for target terms | Weekly | Any rank tracker |
| Organic traffic and conversions by landing page | Monthly | Web analytics |
| Queries driving each page (find new terms to add) | Monthly | Google Search Console |
| AI assistant appearances and descriptions for ~20 questions | Weekly or monthly | Manual grid or an AI visibility tool |
| Revenue or pipeline from organic | Quarterly | CRM attribution |

Optimise for qualified traffic. A page with 100 visitors who sign up beats a page with 10,000 who don't.

SEO and AI search are two surfaces, not a replacement. Work on both, and shift weight toward AI answers as your buyers use them more.
