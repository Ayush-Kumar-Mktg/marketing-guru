# Conversion, landing pages and onboarding

Use this file for landing page audits, homepage and product page structure, checkout, reviews, pricing pages, onboarding and activation. For the words themselves, use the copywriting skill.

---

## 1. The conversion equation

Every page is fighting three forces:

> **Conversion = desire − effort − confusion**

- Raise **desire**: show the outcome, the people who already love it, the thing that makes it different.
- Cut **effort**: fewer fields, fewer clicks, fewer decisions.
- Cut **confusion**: one clear message, one clear next step, familiar layouts.

When a page underperforms, ask which of the three is broken before touching anything.

---

## 2. Seven-step landing page audit

Run these in order. Most pages fail by step 3.

1. **Five-second test.** Show the page to someone who has never seen it for five seconds. They should be able to say what it is, who it's for and why they'd care. If not, fix the hero before anything else.
2. **Headline and subheadline work as a pair.** The headline makes a claim. The subheadline answers "how?", "for whom?" or "what exactly?". It never repeats the headline in other words.
3. **Proof sits next to the claim.** If you say "saves 5 hours a week", the quote about getting Fridays back goes right there, not three screens down.
4. **CTA says what happens.** "Start free trial, no card" or "Book a 20-minute demo", not "Get started".
5. **Friction count.** List every field, step and decision before the user gets value. For each one ask: do we need this now? Every extra ask costs a slice of conversions.
6. **Phone check.** Open it on an actual phone. Does the hero fit? Is the CTA visible without scrolling? Do images and proof render properly? Most pages are designed on a laptop and visited on a phone.
7. **Speed.** Aim for under two seconds. Compressing images, using modern formats, lazy-loading and cutting unused scripts fix most speed problems. Deleting things fixes more than optimising them.

## 3. The first screen

### Messaging
See the copywriting skill (playbook sections 3 to 5). In short: specific benefit, a hook (bold claim or killed objection), the right audience, a subheadline that explains how, and a CTA that continues the story.

### Design
- **Be boring with layout, interesting with product.** Visitors already know how websites work. Unusual layouts make them think about navigation instead of your offer. Save the avant-garde for brands that sell taste (fashion, architecture, agencies).
- **Images must prove value.** Show the product working: a dashboard in use, a short loop of the main action, the item in someone's hands. Decorative images that don't explain anything compete with the headline.
- **Physical products:** show use cases and close-ups of quality. If the packaging is beautiful, show it, since people imagine the unboxing.
- **Negative space.** Give the headline room. Crowded heroes read as cheap.
- **Navigation:** two to four links plus one standout CTA on a homepage or landing page. Every extra link is a new way to wander off.
- **Video in the hero** only if people can't understand the product without seeing it move, and never at the cost of pushing the CTA below the fold.
- **Hard-to-explain products:** let the first CTA scroll people down to the explanation, then ask for the conversion lower down.

## 4. Below the fold

Arrange sections by the questions a buyer asks, in the order they ask them. A typical flow:

1. What is it and who is it for? (hero)
2. Can I trust you? (logos, ratings, guarantee)
3. How does it work? (three steps, minimal text)
4. What do I get? (features framed as outcomes)
5. Do people like me love it? (testimonials beside a CTA)
6. What if it doesn't work for me? (refund policy, FAQ, objections)
7. Final CTA

Repeat the CTA every few sections so nobody has to scroll back up. Put the guarantee early when risk is the main objection.

## 5. Product pages (ecommerce)

- Use the standard layout: images left and CTA right on desktop, images then CTA on mobile. Creativity goes into photos and copy, not layout.
- Photos do the selling. Show the product in use, in context, from several angles, with a short clip if motion matters.
- Write the description for the user's life, not the spec sheet. Feature → benefit → why it matters.
- Keep details in expandable sections so the CTA stays visible.
- Show reviews on the page, ideally near the add-to-cart button.
- Suggest genuinely related items with a one-tap add.
- For consumables, offer a subscription. Group or family subscriptions add both growth (people recruit friends) and retention (leaving costs your friends money).
- Be upfront about returns. Hidden policies feel like traps.

## 6. Checkout: design for gut feelings

Your team sees a logical sequence of necessary steps. The buyer sees a series of chances to quit. At every screen they're asking: is this still worth it?

### Before they start ("Does this look safe? Will it be a pain?")
- Conventional layout, familiar payment logos and visible security signals. This is not the place to be creative.
- Show accepted payment methods immediately. If their preferred one is missing, they leave.
- Show a progress indicator ("Shipping → Payment → Done"). Three clear steps feel shorter than seven small ones.
- Summarise the cart with images and prices.
- **No surprise costs.** Shipping, taxes and fees belong on the product page. Surprises at checkout are the top reason people abandon.
- **Guest checkout by default.** Offer account creation after payment, when they're relaxed.

### While they're filling it in ("Should I stop?")
- Remove every field you don't strictly need right now. Collect the rest later.
- Default sensible values (country from location, most common options pre-selected).
- Address autocomplete, example formats in fields, social sign-in if an account is truly required.
- Validate inline and kindly ("Phone numbers here have 10 digits"), and never wipe the form on an error.
- Offer wallets and pay-later options for bigger baskets. Keep payment on your site where possible.
- Hide the promo code box behind a small "Have a code?" link. A big empty field makes people leave to hunt for discounts.

### At the end ("How do I feel about this?")
People remember the peak and the ending more than the average. A smooth flow with a cold ending feels worse than a clunky flow with a warm ending.

- Show a full summary before the final click. Label the button "Place order", not "Submit".
- The confirmation page says what happens next and when, shows the order number and links to tracking.
- Don't hit them with a referral ask on the confirmation page. Save it for after the product arrives and they're happy.
- Send the confirmation email immediately and make it sound like a person ("Your new kettle is on its way").
- Treat the ending as the start of a relationship: "Welcome", "Here's what to expect".

### Quick checkout audit
1. Open checkout in a private window. Would you trust it with your card if you'd never heard of the brand?
2. Count every field and choice. Time yourself. Remove or default what you can.
3. Read the confirmation page and email out loud. Do they feel like closure?

Fix the biggest drop-off first, test, then move to the next.

## 7. Reviews and social proof that sell

Reviews only work if the product is good. Assuming it is:

- **Comparison reviews persuade more.** "Better than the one I had before" beats "really good". Negative comparisons hurt less than flat complaints, because readers put them down to taste. Prompt for them: "How does this compare to what you used before?"
- **Experts vs customers.** If anyone can judge quality (food, clothes, hotels), show customer reviews. If quality needs expertise (software, finance, healthcare, agencies), lead with expert or peer endorsements.
- **Ask at the right moment.** Ask after the customer has had time to use it, or right after a usage milestone in software. Never interrupt them mid-task.
- **Incentives raise volume and sentiment**, but follow platform rules and disclose them where required.
- **Imperfect is believable.** A near-perfect score with a few honest 3s and 4s converts better than a wall of 5s. A silly one-star review ("didn't like the colour of the box") can even help.
- **The first reviews set the tone.** Launch to your happiest customers first. If an early review is bad, fix the customer's problem fast.
- **Order matters.** Show a strong positive review first. Most people read very few reviews before deciding.
- **Reply to reviews**, especially bad ones. It can turn the reviewer around and shows everyone else you care.
- **Show social engagement** (likes, faces of real customers) near the product where it's genuine.

## 8. Small psychological levers

- **Odd beats logical.** People don't respond to the "rational" variation as often as you'd expect. A strange envelope, a weird visual or an unexpected detail often outperforms the sensible benefit. Test at least one variation that makes no logical sense.
- **Gaze direction.** Faces in images pull attention. A person looking at the product or the CTA makes the viewer look there too.
- **Mystery motivates.** A surprise bonus or mystery item often drives more action than a known reward of the same value.
- **Urgency, honestly.** Real deadlines and real stock limits lift conversion. Fake countdowns destroy trust, and stacking every urgency trick on one page feels like a pushy salesperson.
- **Live chat** helps buyers who have one question blocking the purchase. Keep it small and out of the way of the CTA.
- **Pop-ups:** offer something worth it (discount, giveaway, early access), trigger after real engagement (time on page or a product view), and show them rarely.

The marketing-psychology skill has the full list of biases and how to use them.

## 9. Pricing pages and choices

- **Opinionated defaults, easy to change.** Users want a recommendation, not a menu. Make the most common choice the default and make overriding it obvious but quiet.
- **Highlight one plan.** Don't present equal tiers. Mark the recommended plan visually and say who it's for.
- **One CTA per email or page section.** More options slow people down.
- **Measure your defaults.** If most people change a default, it's the wrong default. If most keep it, you've found the right recommendation.

## 10. Onboarding and activation

Most churn happens before users ever get value. The gap between sign-up and the first "oh, this is useful" moment is where products quietly lose people.

### Find the moment
The "aha moment" is specific: "they did X and saw Y", not "they understood the product". Compare your best long-term users with people who churned. What did the good ones do early that the others didn't? That action is your moment. Turn it into an activation metric, for example "connected their calendar in the first session" or "invited one teammate within a week".

### Build backwards from it
Every onboarding step that doesn't lead to that moment is a candidate for deletion. Common offenders:

- setup steps before any value
- "required" fields that aren't
- tutorials before the user has context
- forced team invites before the user has tried anything

### Four kinds of friction
1. **Not knowing what to do next.** Fix with clearer copy, explicit next steps, progress indicators.
2. **Knowing but it takes too long.** Fix with defaults, templates, autofill, fewer fields.
3. **Not believing it's worth it.** Fix by showing the result before asking for effort, using previews and sample output.
4. **It breaks.** That's an engineering fix, not a copy fix.

### Never show an empty product
A blank dashboard is the worst first screen. Use sample data, templates, a guided first entry or a preview of what it looks like when full.

### A good sequence
1. Something concrete in the first 30 seconds.
2. A real first win, not just finishing a profile.
3. A glimpse of what full use looks like.
4. Only then, ask for the deeper investment: integrations, data, invites.

Most products do this in reverse and lose people before step 3.

---

## 11. Testing

- Test one meaningful change at a time: headline, hero image, CTA copy, page order, offer.
- Wait for enough traffic before calling a winner. Small samples lie.
- Prettier isn't always better. Measure.
- Keep a log of every test, the hypothesis and the result, so you stop re-testing old ideas.
