# Email, lifecycle and personalisation playbook

Deep reference for the email-lifecycle skill: every email type, lifecycle flows, picking an email tool and personalisation.

---

## 1. What every email has to answer

Before writing a single line, answer three questions in plain words:

1. What am I offering?
2. Why does this reader care right now?
3. What is the one thing they should do next?

If any answer is fuzzy, the email will be fuzzy. Most bad emails are bad because the sender never decided what the email was for.

**Every send is a relationship touch.** Receipts, shipping updates and password resets get the highest open rates you will ever see. A shipping email that sounds like a person ("Your kettle left the warehouse this morning") does more for the brand than most campaigns.

---

## 2. Broadcasts vs automated flows

| Type | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Broadcast | Sent once, to a list or segment, by a human pressing send | Newsletter, launch announcement, sale, event invite |
| Automated flow | Triggered by behaviour or time, runs on its own | Welcome series, onboarding, abandoned cart, post-purchase, win-back |

Flows do the compounding. You write them once and they work every day. Broadcasts keep the list warm and carry news.

### The four flows almost every business needs

1. **Welcome.** Sent the moment someone joins. Highest attention you will ever get from that person.
2. **Abandoned cart or abandoned signup.** People who were one step away. Usually the most profitable flow you own, since most carts never check out.
3. **Post-purchase or post-activation.** Help them get value, then ask for a review or referral once they have it.
4. **Win-back.** People who drifted. Remind them why they came, give them a reason to return.

For abandoned carts, answer "why buy now?" while intent is still high: a small incentive, low stock that is real, or simply the reminder plus reassurance about returns.

## 3. The four parts of a single email

### Subject line: earns the open
- Has a hook so it doesn't blend in with the 40 other emails that morning.
- Short enough to survive a phone screen (under ~40 characters is safe).
- Self-evident. Nobody wants to guess why you're writing.
- One emoji can lift attention. One in every email trains people to ignore it.
- The "From" name matters as much as the subject. "Priya from [Brand]" reads like a person. Use different senders for different jobs (founder for stories, support for help, team for news).

Subject patterns that work:
- **The specific pain.** "Was the app slow for you?" works on everyone who felt that.
- **The relevant noun.** Words your audience uses daily ("standup", "month-end close", "GST filing") feel personal without a merge tag.
- **The tease.** "Your price dropped" makes people click to see by how much.
- **The odd reward.** A made-up badge for being inactive gets more curiosity than "We miss you".

### Design: decides read, skim or delete
People decide in about a second. Help them:
- Simple layout, plenty of white space, readable font size.
- Mobile first. Most email is read on phones, and a mobile layout scales up to desktop far better than the reverse.
- Big tap-friendly buttons.
- Small image files. Heavy images on weak phones mean slow loads and deletes.
- Send a test to your own phone before every send.
- Use a button colour that appears nowhere else in the email so the eye goes straight to it.
- Simple illustrations often explain a feature better than a cluttered screenshot.
- GIFs and short motion add interest for visual products. Make a boring chore (cleaning, setup, packing) look fun.

### Body copy: delivers on the subject
- Pay off the promise the subject made in the first line.
- Aggressively short. Every sentence earns its place or goes.
- Benefits in scenes, features in support. "Heat and eat in 3 minutes, zero washing up" beats "chef-prepared meals".
- Comparison works. Show the old way next to your way.
- Real customer quotes and photos beat polished ones.
- Teach one thing at a time in onboarding. Pick the single most important first step and stop there.
- Talk about what the product does for them, not how it works under the hood. Nobody new cares about the API.

### Goal: one email, one job
- A promo drives a purchase. A nurture email drives one read. A win-back drives one return visit.
- One clear CTA in almost every case.
- Exception: a welcome email can offer a short "choose your path" menu, since you don't yet know what the new subscriber wants.
- Exception: a big sale event can carry several products, because the reader is already in a buying mood.

## 4. Email types, with what makes each one land

**Welcome.** State who you are and why you exist in the first paragraph. Communicate values, not your founding story. "Welcome to the team" only works if your community is a team. Don't jam in everything. One goal. Use the dead space at the bottom for a testimonial or referral nudge instead of a pile of links.

**Onboarding.** One concept per email. Show two quick wins: one personal, one work-related, so people see the product fits their whole life. Video plus a simple graphic plus three lines of copy covers every learning style.

**Nurture.** Give them something useful: a report, a template, a case study. A three-sentence customer story with a strong photo can outsell a long product pitch. Ask yourself: what asset makes us look like the authority and helps users get more from the product?

**Promotion.** Lead with the offer and the value in the headline. Position yourself against the alternative. Be upfront about extra costs like shipping, since surprises at checkout kill conversion. Put the deadline next to the button.

**Free trial offer.** Pair each feature with its benefit. A generous trial (longer than the category norm) plus a real deadline creates urgency without pressure. Let the final CTA stand alone, with no paragraph slowing it down.

**Win-back.**
- Call out the likely reason they left ("Too slow? We spent four months on it.").
- Write one win-back per top pain point.
- Reference something they did ("You installed us 6 months ago"). It reminds them why they signed up.
- Use usage data: hours saved, tasks done, streaks.
- Keep it playful. Desperate win-backs read as desperate.
- A deep discount makes sense for digital products where the marginal cost is close to zero.

**Abandoned cart.** Show the item. Give a reason to finish. "Reveal new price" as the button turns the click into a small commitment, so they land primed to buy. Rewrite the default template copy your tool ships with. Everyone can tell it's a template.

**Newsletter with products.** Content first, product second, both on the same theme. A guide to drinking black coffee followed by the three roasts that taste best black works. Cut anything off-theme (gift cards, category grids) that dilutes it.

## 5. Lifecycle stack: match the tool to the operator

Most teams either under-power their lifecycle (one basic tool, no segments) or over-buy (an enterprise platform with 90% of features untouched). Pick the tier that matches who will run it:

| Tier | Who runs it | Approach | Stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Starter** | A generalist marketer or founder | Template sequences, a few manual segments, simple triggers | Before product-market fit |
| **Intermediate** | One or two people who own lifecycle | Behavioural triggers, A/B tests, dynamic segments | After fit, growing team |
| **Advanced** | A dedicated lifecycle team plus data support | Predictive segments, cross-channel orchestration, custom data pipelines | Scale-up |

Diagnostic questions before buying anything:
1. How many people will actively work in this tool every week?
2. Can we maintain custom data pipelines, or will they rot?
3. What share of our current automations are running and working?
4. Is the current stack producing results, or are we just maintaining it?

The classic mistake is buying advanced tooling for a starter team because the sales demo was impressive. The result is expensive licences, broken automations and a program that underperforms a simpler setup. A small team moving to a simpler tool often sees results go up, because they finally have time to write good emails.

Match the tool to the people you have, not the team you hope to have.

## 6. Story before channel

When a channel "doesn't work", the problem is usually the story. Ads tell one version, the founder pitches another, the website a third and onboarding emails a fourth. Everything looks on brand but nothing lines up, so people leak out at every handoff:

- Ads get clicks but pages don't convert.
- Pages get trials but trials don't pay.
- Trials attract the wrong people.
- Churn feels random.

### Find the core story
Ask: "If we vanished tomorrow, what would our best customers miss most?" The answer is a feeling, not a feature.

- A file sync tool → "my stuff is wherever I am"
- A scheduling tool → "no more back-and-forth"
- A store builder → "my business exists online"

Then:
1. Pull 10 to 20 real customer quotes from sales calls, support tickets, reviews (yours and competitors') and community threads.
2. Highlight repeated phrases: complaints in their words, outcomes they brag about, the enemy they rant about.
3. Write one line in their words: "We exist so [person] can finally [outcome] without [pain]."
4. Compare it to your homepage, your ads and your sales deck. The gap between them is your story problem.

Worked example (expense software for mid-size finance teams):
- Surface story: "Automated expense reporting with real-time controls."
- What customers say: "I hate chasing receipts." "I only find problems when month-end is already ruined." "I want to trust the numbers without policing people."
- Core story: "Finance teams trust spending without chasing anyone or getting surprised at month-end."

Story drift is cheap early, when friends and early adopters fill in the gaps. It gets expensive as you reach colder audiences, hire more people and add features. Fixing the story improves every funnel stage at once.

Keep a single living story doc that every email, ad and page pulls from: core story, personas in their own language, the deeper problems behind their pains, value props competitors can't copy, and hooks mapped to top, middle and bottom of funnel.

## 7. Personalisation that doesn't feel creepy

People expect brands to remember them. They get annoyed when brands don't. They also get uneasy when brands know too much. Personalise with data they knowingly gave you.

### Ground rules
- **First-party data first.** What customers tell you directly (forms, surveys, preferences) and what they do on your own site, app and emails. It is the most accurate and the least creepy, and privacy rules make third-party data less usable every year.
- **Say what you collect and why.** Transparency buys permission.
- **Collect only what you'll use.** Every extra field is a cost and a risk.
- **Don't overstep.** One creepy message can undo a year of trust.
- **Keep data clean.** Bad data compounds. Different teams tracking the same thing differently, logged-in vs logged-out users and multiple devices all create mess. If data quality isn't improving, it's getting worse.

### Before picking tools, answer
- What business outcome are we after?
- Which metric tracks it?
- Which tools will act on it?
- What data must we collect to power those tools?
- Which segments do we want, and what data defines them?

A CRM organises contacts for sales and marketing. A customer data platform pulls events from everywhere (site, app, email, support) into one profile per person. Most early teams don't need the second. Get a clean analytics setup first.

### Tactics by channel

**Website**
- Personalise search results with known preferences (size, category, past purchases).
- Hide out-of-stock items in the shopper's size and sort by their favourite categories.
- Only show upsells to customers whose past orders show they'll buy more.
- Give fit guidance from purchase and return history ("Most people like you size up in this one").

**Email**
- Segment by behaviour: one-time buyers get different offers from regulars, weekend buyers get weekend offers.
- Ask role and team size at signup, then send role-specific onboarding (solo users get solo tips, teams get collaboration tips).
- Trigger the next email from the feature they just explored.

**Ads**
- Match retargeting to the content someone read. A person who read your post on knee pain for runners sees a runners' ad, not a generic one.
- Test several reasons-to-return for lapsed customers (convenience, delivery, a local pop-up). People drift for logistics, not always dislike.
- Keep exclusion lists updated automatically so you stop paying to show ads to existing customers.

**In-app and SMS**
- Ask for reviews and referrals based on engagement plus timing: after a streak, a finished level, a renewal, a delivered big order. Never mid-task.
- Use location triggers carefully (a customer visiting a city where you have a store).
- Spot users who are struggling (fewer results than average) and send help or a nudge before they quit.

**Support**
- Route high-value customers to humans fast.
- Show agents the full history so customers never repeat themselves.
- Flag people with repeated tickets for proactive training.

## 8. Email audit checklist

- [ ] Can I say what this email is for in one sentence?
- [ ] Does the subject line hook and make sense on a phone?
- [ ] Does the "From" name sound like a person?
- [ ] Does the first line pay off the subject?
- [ ] Is there one primary CTA, with a button colour used nowhere else?
- [ ] Did I check it on my own phone?
- [ ] Is every extra cost or condition visible?
- [ ] Have I cut the bottom third of filler links?
- [ ] Is this send triggered by behaviour, or just by the calendar?
- [ ] Is the story here the same story the ads and homepage tell?
